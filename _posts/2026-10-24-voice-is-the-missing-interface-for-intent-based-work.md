title: "Voice Is the Better Interface"

I type a lot. Chat windows, coding agents, terminal prompts — most of my day is typing instructions into boxes. Dictation was supposed to fix that. It doesn't.

Speaking into a text box feels wrong in a way that's hard to explain until you've felt it. You have to compose in writing while speaking: monitor yourself for clean sentences, edit in real time. It costs more energy than typing, not less. And something is always lost.

What's lost is everything that isn't words. In a real conversation, people hear your tone, your breath, your laugh, your hesitation. You can trail off, say "wait, no," change direction mid-sentence, and a human listener absorbs all of it. A transcript box just punishes it. The tone, the laugh, the pause — those aren't decoration. They carry meaning: urgency, doubt, humor, fatigue. They tell the other side what you actually mean.

This matters more now because the work itself is changing. With a coding agent or a chat assistant, you no longer give step-by-step instructions. You describe roughly what you want and let the agent figure out the rest. The work is intent-based now.

But "roughly" is exactly what text is bad at. Text forces you to either over-specify or be misunderstood. Humans don't communicate intent in words alone — we communicate it in how we say the words. The hesitation says "I'm unsure about this part." The laugh says "don't take that literally." The tired voice says "just make it work, don't ask me ten questions." An agent that only reads text is deaf to all of that, so it has to interrogate you with clarifying questions — the very friction intent-based work was supposed to remove.

The technology has quietly crossed an important line here. The old voice pipeline was speech-to-text, then a language model, then text-to-speech — and information died at every step. The new native speech-to-speech models take audio in and audio out directly, carrying both the meaning and the sound of what you said through a single model. I asked Gemini Live once whether it could tell I was laughing. It said yes — because the laugh never had to become the word "laugh" to reach the model.

But let's be honest about where this stands: the plumbing is solved, the listening isn't. Today's voice models sound human. They don't yet listen like humans. No real backchannels, no natural overlap, no adapting to the fact that you're joking or exhausted. And they mostly live in the cloud, while the rhythm of real conversation needs to happen close to you, on the device.

So voice is not a convenience feature, not another input method to bolt onto a chat box. For intent-based work, it is the interface. The agent that understands what I mean — not just what I said — will be the one that can hear me.
